Add overhead satellite passes, with SGP4 running on the phone

Tells you which satellites are about to come over, on what frequency, how high
they will get, which way to look and what Doppler to chase. There is no server, so
this cannot call a prediction API: the orbit propagator has to be in the page.

assets/sgp4.js is SGP4 from scratch, near-Earth half only, against WGS-72 constants
because TLE mean elements are fitted with WGS-72 and feeding the model WGS-84 makes
the answer worse rather than more modern. SDP4 is absent on purpose. Everything that
needs it has a period of 225 minutes or more, meaning geosynchronous or Molniya, and
nothing in that class is workable with a 5 W handheld and a rubber duck, so init()
reports deep space and the caller drops it rather than propagating it badly.

A wrong propagator does not look wrong. It returns a confident time and a confident
bearing, and you find out by standing outside pointing at empty sky. So it is
measured against two independent references rather than reviewed: Vallado's
published verification vectors, where 158 near-Earth state vectors agree to 7.3e-9
km and all 24 deep-space cases are correctly identified, and wheretheiss.at, a
separate implementation on its own elements, where the ISS ground position agrees to
7.8 km. The microns only prove the terms are typed correctly; the 7.8 km is the
meaningful figure and is about one second of ISS travel, so clock and element skew.
The fixtures are committed so the check is hermetic offline.

One fixture shaped the code. Catalogue number 28872 has a perigee 51 km below the
surface and is mid-re-entry; the reference propagates it for 50 minutes before
failing. An added sub-surface-perigee rejection made that case fail, so it was
removed - matching the reference is what lets the vectors be used unmodified. Decay
is a question about the data, and import-tle.mjs answers it there.

The elements are automated and the frequencies are not, which is the whole design.
Elements are a fit to a short arc: good to a kilometre for a day or two, and by a
week out wrong enough to move a pass by minutes. That decays on its own, so a daily
Action refreshes it and the importer only ever rewrites the tle fields, by line-level
surgery, for the same reason make-odds.mjs does.

Which satellites are worth tuning cannot be automated, because the databases answer
a different question. SatNOGS tracks what a satellite has ever carried: 29
transmitters for the ISS, among them Soyuz suit channels and a Progress beacon from
the nineties. Its frequencies are not trustworthy either - it gives NOAA 19 as
137.932 where the APT downlink was 137.100.

Which led to the finding that mattered. The NOAA APT birds are the classic beginner
catch on 137 MHz, and all three were decommissioned during 2025 - NOAA 18 in June,
NOAA 19 and NOAA 15 in August - with APT now transmitted by nothing. Celestrak still
publishes their elements. Had these been added from memory, the feature would have
shipped three prominent entries confidently predicting passes for three silent
satellites. Orbital data says where something is, never whether it is transmitting.
They are excluded, the reasoning is recorded in data/sat.json, and the suite asserts
those catalogue numbers stay out, because they are exactly what a well-meaning later
change adds back.

The panel stays hidden until you share a position. A region centre would give times
that look authoritative and are wrong by however far away you are, and a confidently
wrong timetable is worse than none. Only passes above 10 degrees are offered.

The suite caught one bug of mine: a whole sentence in a .tag, which is nowrap, so
the panel overflowed a 320px screen by 32px. The sentence is now a paragraph.
